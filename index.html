Prep & Aftercare

Before Your Appointment

Get Ready for Your Takedown

  • Leave your style as it is. Don't start removing braids yourself — partially removed styles tangle and take longer to finish safely.
  • Clear a comfortable spot with a chair, good light, and a little room to work — a living room or kitchen corner is perfect.
  • Have a towel and a trash bag handy for extension hair as it comes out.
  • Plan your time honestly. Check your service duration when you book and avoid scheduling anything tight right after.
  • Tell us about tender spots, scalp sensitivity, or any areas that feel sore so we can adjust our technique.
  • For kids: a favorite show, snack, and toy nearby helps little ones settle in for their session.

After Your Takedown

Care for Your Natural Hair

  • Expect shed hair — it's normal. You shed 50–100 strands a day; weeks of braids means weeks of shed hair released at once.
  • Deep condition within the first week to restore moisture after weeks under a protective style.
  • Be gentle for a few days. Your scalp may feel tender; avoid tight styles and heavy manipulation right away.
  • Consider a trim. Ends that were protected for weeks often reveal splits — a light trim keeps growth on track.
  • Moisturize daily: a light leave-in and oil on the ends keeps newly freed hair soft between washes.
  • Give your hair a breather — ideally one to two weeks — before reinstalling a long-wear protective style.

Braid Takedown Questions, Answered

Everything Atlanta clients ask us before booking — answered honestly, the way we'd want it explained to us.

How long does a braid takedown take?+

It depends on the size, length, and age of your style. Jumbo braids can come down in about an hour or two, medium knotless braids typically take two to three hours, and small or tiny braids can take four hours or more. Our signature packages run 3h 30min for the Signature Luxury Takedown, 4h 30min for the Boho/Miracle Knots Recovery Takedown Package, and 5h 30min for the Ultimate Luxury Takedown Experience. When you book, we give you a realistic time window so you can plan your day around it.

Does a takedown hurt?+

A proper braid takedown should not hurt. You may feel light tugging as each braid is unraveled, and your scalp can feel tender for a day or two afterward simply because it has carried the weight of a style for weeks. What you should never feel is sharp pulling, yanking, or scissors near your own hair. We work slowly, support each section as we remove it, and check in with you throughout — if something feels uncomfortable, tell us and we adjust immediately.

How long should I keep braids in before a takedown?+

For most people, six to eight weeks is the sweet spot for box braids, knotless braids, and boho styles. By then your hair has grown roughly an inch, shed hair has accumulated at the roots, and the style has loosened enough that removal is clean and gentle. Smaller braids and finer hair types usually do better closer to six weeks. If your scalp is itching persistently, your edges feel sore, or you can see buildup at the roots, those are signs your takedown is due regardless of the calendar.

What happens if I leave braids in too long?+

Past eight to ten weeks, the shed hair trapped at your roots starts to mat and lock together, and product buildup hardens around the base of each braid. Removal then takes much longer, requires far more detangling, and carries a real risk of breakage — in severe cases the matted sections have to be cut out. Styles left in too long can also stress your edges and hairline from constant tension as the braids grow out and hang heavier. Booking your takedown on time is the single easiest way to protect the length you've grown.

What is a loc comb-out, and how long does it take?+

A loc comb-out reverses mature locs back into loose natural hair by picking each loc apart strand by strand with a metal comb, water, and conditioner — no cutting required. Because years of shed hair are woven into every loc, the process is slow, meticulous work that commonly takes four hours or longer depending on the number, size, and age of your locs. Every week, we meet clients in Atlanta who lost length — sometimes inches of it — to a rushed, rough takedown. The frustrating part is that almost all of that damage is preventable. Braid removal is not complicated, but it is unforgiving of shortcuts. This is the step-by-step method behind every professional braid takedown we perform, written so you can follow it at home or recognize quality work when you book it.

What you'll need

  • Small scissors — for cutting extension hair only, never near your own strands
  • A rattail comb — for loosening braid bases and teasing out buildup
  • A wide-tooth comb and a detangling brush
  • A spray bottle with water and a little leave-in conditioner
  • Clips to keep finished sections separate from unfinished ones
  • A trash bag for extension hair — there will be more than you expect

Step 1: Work in sections, always

Divide your head into four to six zones and finish one completely before moving on. The single biggest cause of breakage in a DIY braid takedown is a half-finished head: loose natural hair tangles against braids that are still installed, and the two textures mat together. However you take down braids — knotless braid takedown, box braid removal, boho styles — the rule is the same: one braid, start to finish, then the next.

Step 2: Cut the extensions — safely

Find where your natural hair ends inside the braid. A reliable guide: your hair is usually no longer than the point where the braid's texture changes and the extension continues on its own. Cut at least an inch or two below that point. If you're unsure where your hair ends — and with knotless braids the feed-in makes it genuinely hard to tell — cut lower and unravel more by hand. Hair grows back; a bad cut at the wrong spot costs you months.

Step 3: Unravel and release the shed hair

Unravel each braid with your fingers, then gently separate the strands. At the base you'll find a buildup of shed hair, lint, and product — this is normal. You shed roughly 50 to 100 hairs a day, and after six weeks in braids, that's thousands of hairs released in one sitting. Loosen the buildup plug with the rattail comb and your fingers before any water touches your head. Wetting matted shed hair first is how takedowns turn into cut-it-out emergencies.

Step 4: Detangle each section before washing

Mist the section, add a little conditioner for slip, and detangle from the ends upward with the wide-tooth comb. Only when a section is fully detangled should it be clipped away and the next braid started. When the whole head is done, detangle once more overall — patiently.

Step 5: Wash and condition like you mean it

Start with a clarifying shampoo to lift weeks of buildup, follow with a moisturizing shampoo, then deep condition for at least 20 to 30 minutes. Rinse, apply leave-in, and seal the ends with a light oil. Your hair has been protected but also untouched for weeks — this wash sets the tone for everything after.

The honest shortcut: This process takes most people three to six hours on their own head, working at angles that tire your arms and hide your own hairline from view. A mobile braid takedown in Atlanta puts a specialist's hands — and eyes — on every section. If your braids are small, old, or matted at the base, that's the appointment to book rather than brave it alone.

Ask five braiders how long braids can stay in and you'll hear everything from four weeks to four months. After years of performing braid takedown in Atlanta — and seeing the condition of hair at every possible interval — we can tell you the 6–8 week rule isn't a sales tactic. It's where the biology of your hair lands. Here's the reasoning, so you can judge your own style with clear eyes.

Your hair keeps growing — and shedding — under there

Hair grows about half an inch a month. At six weeks, you have roughly three-quarters of an inch of new growth loosening every braid at the root. Meanwhile you've shed around 3,000 to 4,000 hairs that had nowhere to go — they're woven into the base of your braids, tangling a little more each day. Up to about eight weeks, that shed hair releases cleanly during braid removal. Past it, the shed hair starts to knot around your living hair, and the root of each braid becomes a small mat that has to be picked apart strand by strand.

Buildup compounds quietly

Sweat, sebum, edge control, scalp oils, and environmental dust collect at the base of every braid. Washing helps, but braided hair never rinses as clean as loose hair. By week eight, most clients have a visible ring of buildup at each root. Left longer, that buildup hardens, traps moisture against the scalp, and creates the itching and flaking that many people mistake for a scalp condition — when what they really need is a takedown and a proper wash.

Traction risk rises as braids age

A fresh braid distributes its weight close to the scalp. As your hair grows, the same heavy braid hangs from a longer, thinner lever of new growth — and new growth at the hairline is the most fragile hair you have. This is why edges suffer most in weeks nine, ten, and beyond: the style hasn't changed, but the physics have. Persistent soreness, small bumps at the hairline, or braids that feel like they're "pulling" are your scalp filing a formal complaint.

So when exactly should you book?

  • Fine hair, small braids, knotless styles: plan your knotless braid takedown at six weeks.
  • Medium and jumbo box braids on healthy hair: six to eight weeks is a safe window.
  • Any style: book sooner if you see heavy buildup, feel ongoing tension, or notice braids slipping significantly at the root.

And one myth worth retiring: leaving braids in longer does not give you "more growth." Your hair grows at the same rate either way. What a timely takedown protects is retention — the length you already grew, kept instead of broken off in a marathon removal session.

Running past eight weeks already? Don't panic and don't soak your head. Book a professional braid removal so the matted bases can be released dry, section by section, before any water touches them. That's the difference between a long appointment and lost hair.

The most common question we hear about locs is some version of: "Do I really have to cut them off?" For most people, the answer is no. A loc comb-out — also called a loc takedown — reverses locs back into loose natural hair by hand. It's slow, it's detailed, and when it's done properly, clients routinely keep six, eight, even ten-plus inches of the hair they grew inside their locs. This guide explains what actually happens during a comb-out, so you can decide if it's right for you.

What the process actually looks like

Each loc is saturated with water and conditioner until it's soft and pliable. Then, starting from the tip, your specialist works a strong metal comb into the loc a few millimeters at a time, teasing apart the woven matrix of hair, and combing out the freed strands before moving up another few millimeters. This repeats — hundreds of times per loc — until the entire loc is loose hair again. The loosened hair is detangled continuously as we go; letting combed-out hair sit in a pile is how it re-mats.

Why it takes hours

A loc isn't a braid you can unravel. It's years of shed hair — remember, hair that would normally fall away daily — compacted and interlocked with your living hair. A single mature loc can hold a surprising volume of hair, and every centimeter of it must be opened by hand. A full head typically takes four hours at minimum, and longer, thicker, or very mature locs can run well beyond that, sometimes across more than one session. Anyone quoting you a full comb-out in ninety minutes is either planning to cut or planning to rush — and rushed comb-outs are where the breakage happens.

What to expect: the shedding is real

Be prepared for what looks like an alarming amount of loose hair. Most of it is years of shed hair that was locked inside your locs — it was never going to stay on your head. Your remaining loose hair will likely feel thinner than you remember for the first week or two, then settle as it gets washed, conditioned, and trimmed. A good trim after a comb-out is not optional in our book: the oldest ends, which lived at the tips of your locs, are usually ready to go.

The takedown is only half the appointment. What you do in the seven days after your braid removal decides whether your hair comes out of its protective style stronger — or spends the next month recovering. This is the first-week routine we walk every Untwine Atelier client through, whether we just finished their braid takedown in Atlanta or they're caring for hair at home.

Day 1: The clarifying wash

After weeks of buildup at the roots, a regular shampoo won't cut it. Start with a clarifying shampoo to lift product residue, sweat, and sebum, then follow with a moisturizing shampoo so your hair doesn't feel stripped. Work in sections, let the suds do the work, and don't pile your hair on top of your head — that's how freshly detangled hair re-tangles in the shower. (If we handled your takedown, this wash and scalp care were already part of your package — start at Day 2.)

Day 1–2: Deep condition, no shortcuts

Apply a deep conditioner generously, cover with a cap, and give it a full 30 minutes — with gentle heat if you have it. Your hair has been protected from the elements but also from moisture treatments for six to eight weeks, and it drinks this first session up. Rinse cool, then detangle one final time with a wide-tooth comb while the hair is still slippery.

Day 2–3: Assess and trim

With your hair clean, stretched, and detangled, you can finally see it clearly — which makes this the honest moment for a trim. Ends that spent weeks inside braids often show splits or thinning that were invisible in the style. Trimming a quarter to half an inch now prevents those splits from traveling upward. If you're not confident trimming your own hair, book it — this is the single highest-value salon visit in the whole protective-style cycle.

Days 3–7: A simple moisture routine

  • Every day or two: mist with water or a water-based leave-in, smooth a light cream through, and seal the ends with oil.
  • At night: loose twists, a pineapple, or a satin bonnet/pillowcase — cotton pillowcases undo a week of good work.
  • Keep manipulation low: your scalp may still feel tender and your hair is adjusting to being loose. Puffs, twist-outs, and loose buns beat tight styles this week.
  • Skip the heat where you can. If you blow dry, use medium heat and a protectant.

When can you reinstall a protective style?

Give your hair one to two weeks of freedom first — longer if your scalp was sore, your edges look stressed, or the previous style stayed in past eight weeks. Reinstalling knotless braids, box braids, or a sew-in on tired hair is how protective styles stop being protective. When your hair feels soft, your scalp is calm, and your ends are trimmed, you're ready — and your next style will last its own healthy six to eight weeks.
